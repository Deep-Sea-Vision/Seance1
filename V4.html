    MISSION BRIEFING

    Environmental Survey

    You are the onboard AI of a BlueROV2, sent to inspect a stretch of coastline for pollution and a suspected underwater pipeline leak. Your only eyes are an optical camera running the YOLO detection algorithm. The water ahead is dark and murky.

    DEPTH: 200M

    The Light Problem

    The YOLO AI works like a human eye: it needs light to recognize shapes and colors. Right now, the screen is pitch black. How should the AI proceed to find the target?

    GAME OVER

    HULL BREACH

    At 200m there is no sunlight at all. YOLO analyzes a fully black image and reports "nothing", so the ROV keeps moving forward blind and slams into a coral formation at full speed. The impact cracks the housing and floods the electronics.

    Lesson: a camera-based AI cannot see without a light source. No light means no image, and a blind vehicle moving forward is a collision waiting to happen.

    Marine Snow

    The lights are on, but the water is full of floating sand and organic particles called "marine snow" (turbidity). How should you set your lights to see clearly without blinding the AI?

    BACKSCATTER

    The bright light reflects straight back off the floating particles, like high-beam headlights in fog. The image turns into a white wall and YOLO loses the target for a moment before an automatic brightness cutoff kicks in.

    Lesson: in turbid water, strong light aimed straight ahead bounces back into the lens. Angling the light down and lowering its power reduces this "backscatter".

    The False Positive

    An object is floating ahead. YOLO gives a 45% confidence score for "jellyfish" and a 55% score for "plastic bag". Jellyfish have smooth, round bells; this object has sharp, angular edges. What is your final decision?

    SECOND LOOK

    You classified it as an animal and moved on, but the object drifted straight toward the propeller. A collision-avoidance routine kicks in just in time and pulls the ROV back.

    Lesson: YOLO relies on geometry, not just labels. Sharp, angular edges are a strong sign of a man-made object, even when confidence scores are close.

    Camouflage & Geometry

    You must find the underwater pipeline buried in the sand. Nature is chaotic and rarely makes perfectly straight shapes. How does the AI find the hidden pipeline?

    GAME OVER

    LOST AT SEA

    You followed the shadow of a passing manta ray for over a mile, far off the survey line. By the time the AI realizes its mistake, the battery has dropped below the safe return threshold. The ROV cannot make it back to the surface support boat.

    Lesson: living things move unpredictably and rarely form straight lines. Chasing motion instead of shape can burn through a vehicle's most limited resource: its battery.

    MISSION SUCCESS!

    Correct. Human engineering creates perfect straight lines and circles that nature rarely produces. YOLO detects the straight geometry of the pipe under the sand and flags the exact leak location for the survey team.

    You learned how light, water clarity, object geometry, and motion all shape what a computer-vision AI can and cannot see underwater.
    MISSION BRIEFING

    Search & Track

    You are the onboard AI of a military AUV, tasked with locating an enemy submarine somewhere in these waters. You must find it and confirm its identity without being detected yourself.

    Active vs Passive Sonar

    Cameras are useless at this range. "Active sonar" sends a loud PING and listens for the echo. "Passive sonar" only quietly listens to the ocean. You are in enemy territory. How do you search for the target?

    The Jamming Zone

    Warning: the enemy heard your PING instantly and deployed an acoustic jammer. Your sonar screen turns to static and you are effectively blind. How do you keep tracking the enemy?

    GAME OVER

    TORPEDOED

    Visual range underwater is only a few meters, so switching the camera lights on lit you up like a beacon. The enemy submarine, already alerted by your active ping, fires from two kilometers away. There is no time to react.

    Lesson: light travels a very short distance underwater. Once your sonar is jammed, a different sense entirely, like magnetism, is needed to keep tracking a steel hull safely.

    PARTIAL SUCCESS

    You used magnetic sensors to navigate through the jamming and recorded the enemy's acoustic signature before quietly withdrawing. Command is pleased with the intel, though the submarine itself got away.

    Active sonar gives instant range and position, but it also reveals you. A stealthier approach might have avoided the jamming altogether.

    Biological Noise

    The ocean is loud. Passive sonar picks up two sounds: a random "click-click-wooo" and a constant, rhythmic "bzzzzzzz". Which signature is more likely to come from a machine?

    The Whale Cover

    You followed the biological noise: it was a pod of sperm whales. But a short-range sensor picks up a massive metallic shadow directly beneath them. The enemy submarine was hiding its engine noise behind the whale clicks. You are now only 50 meters away.

    GAME OVER

    CRUSHED

    Your AUV weighs about a ton. The submarine weighs several thousand tons of solid steel. The collision does nothing to the hull and destroys your vehicle instantly.

    Lesson: a direct collision only makes sense when your vehicle can survive it. A quiet, non-violent approach is both safer and better suited to a tracking mission.

    STEALTH VICTORY!

    You maneuvered silently and attached a magnetic tracker to their hull without being noticed. Surface command can now follow their movements by satellite the next time they surface. Mission accomplished.

    The Decoy

    The enemy knows you are close. They release a chemical decoy that creates a massive cloud of bubbles. Sonar waves bounce off bubbles almost like a solid wall, and your screen now shows two targets. Which one is the real submarine?

    WASTED SECONDS

    You start closing on the huge target, but its outline is too clean and uniform for solid steel. A quick reassessment tells you it's just gas, not metal, before you commit any further.

    Lesson: a bubble cloud reflects sound well because of the sharp change in density, but it produces no engine noise and no consistent mass. The real target is smaller but active.

    Magnetic Confirmation

    You are 500 meters away and need full confirmation before acting. The suspected vessel is made of thousands of tons of steel. What sensor gives you final proof it's a real submarine and not something else?

    WRONG TOOL

    Water temperature tells you nothing about what the object is made of. The AI wastes a few seconds waiting for a reading that will never help, but the target has not noticed you yet.

    Lesson: thousands of tons of steel create a strong, measurable disturbance in Earth's magnetic field. Temperature sensors cannot detect that at all.

    MISSION SUCCESS!

    Correct. Thousands of tons of steel create a clear disruption in Earth's magnetic field. The MAD sensor confirms the target is metal, not marine life. Identity confirmed, mission complete.

    You learned the difference between active and passive sonar, how decoys and biological noise can mislead a sensor, and how magnetic detection confirms what sound alone cannot.
    MISSION BRIEFING

    Extreme Depth

    You are the onboard AI of a deep-diving submersible, descending toward a wreck several thousand meters down. This scenario is inspired by real deep-sea engineering: the deeper you go, the less forgiving any mistake becomes.

    The Design Flaw

    Most deep-sea subs are perfect spheres made of titanium. This submersible is a cylinder made of carbon fiber, a material that is excellent at resisting pulling forces but far weaker against crushing pressure. What is the main risk of using carbon fiber for a deep dive?

    WRONG PHYSICS

    Carbon fiber is actually very light, not heavy; that is exactly why it is used here. Weight was never the real risk. You are still on the surface, so there is time to review the material's real weakness before diving.

    Lesson: the real danger with carbon fiber is fatigue. Repeated cycles of extreme pressure can quietly weaken its internal layers, long before any visible crack appears.
    DEPTH: 3,200M

    The Acoustic Warning

    At 3,200m, water pressure is 320 times what it is on the surface. The hull's internal microphone picks up a sound: POP... CRACK... What does this acoustic signal mean, and what should the AI do?

    DEPTH: 3,500M — RED ALERT

    The Inevitable End

    Red alert. The cracking sounds are now continuous. The carbon fiber has lost most of its structural strength at 3,500m. As the AI, what is your emergency protocol?

    GAME OVER

    OUTCOME: IMPLOSION

    Whether from continuing past an acoustic warning, or from reaching the point where the hull had already lost its strength, the result here is always the same. Carbon fiber does not fail gradually once fatigue reaches a critical level; under thousands of tons of water pressure, it fails instantly and completely.

    Debrief: the real lesson of this path is timing. Recognizing the true weakness of the material, and taking every acoustic warning seriously, were the only real chances to turn back. Once the point of no return is passed, no protocol can undo it.
